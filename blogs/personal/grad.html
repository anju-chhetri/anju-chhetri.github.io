It all started in 2024, right after I completed my undergraduate studies. I knew early on that I wanted to pursue graduate studies abroad. But with my interest in Machine Learning—and the massive boom in AI and technology, the competition has grown intensely. The number of students opting for computer science–related fields has skyrocketed over the years. As I often joked with my friends, it’s brutal out there.
At the beginning, I wasn’t even sure what path I wanted to take. Master’s or PhD? The US or Europe? While PhD programs often come with funding, I realized I wasn’t yet certain about the specific research area I wanted to commit to long-term. A Master’s felt like the right stepping stone.
The US offers direct PhD programs after undergraduate studies, which was appealing. But I was drawn to Europe. However, Europe’s strict credit requirements made admissions more challenging, especially given my background in electronics rather than pure computer science. Like many others, I initially tried to follow the path of my university’s alumni—aiming straight for a PhD in the US. I began drafting my Statement of Purpose (SOP), knowing it was one of the most critical parts of my application. After writing the first few paragraphs, I shared it with some senior friends. One of them told me bluntly: "This is exactly the kind of SOP people advise you not to submit."
I realized I had fallen into a common trap, starting with generic lines like “I’ve loved computers since childhood…” Instead, I learned that a strong SOP is about clarity, purpose, and storytelling grounded in real experiences. What truly helped me improve was reaching out to people who had already gone through this process. I used resources like WriteIvy and, more importantly, asked a lot of questions. Their insights helped me avoid mistakes I didn’t even know I was making.
At the same time, I became more intentional with my research projects—choosing work that helped me build a coherent narrative about who I am and what I want to pursue. I submitted my first applications for Fall 2025. One of my top choices was UIUC. Yes, aiming high on the first attempt but I couldn’t stop myself from trying. The results were mixed: four rejections and one offer from Oregon State University. That’s when I truly understood the importance of finances. While Oregon State was a strong option, the cost around $60,000 per year (excluding summer) was difficult for me to justify. After careful consideration, I decided not to pursue it.
Meanwhile, I continued working on my research and even submitted a paper to MICCAI, one of the leading conferences in medical imaging. While waiting for results, I applied to several German universities, including TUM, Saarland, FAU, and Freiburg. Given my electronics background, most programs rejected me due to insufficient credits in core computer science subjects except one.
At the Technical University of Munich (TUM), I narrowly passed the first screening based on academic background. The second stage involved an interview and an aptitude test. Eventually, I received an offer.
At one point, I considered applying to many more universities—EPFL, Imperial, and others. But each application came with a cost, often exceeding $100 when factoring in test scores and fees. That’s when I realized something important: Applying broadly might increase your chances, but each “arrow” you shoot has a cost. Being financially strategic matters. To make better decisions, I reached out to professors and students asking them to review my profile and assess my chances. I even applied for fee waivers where possible. During this period, I also emailed professors, still unsure whether to pursue a Master’s or PhD. Between April and June, something shifted.
I began receiving positive responses—from professors at universities like the University of Southampton and the University of Warwick. In total, I exchanged around 30 emails with professors across institutions.
Those conversations boosted my confidence significantly. One key lesson stood out: Reaching out to professors who genuinely share your research interests makes a huge difference. Many of them mentioned that they visited my website or profile before responding: 1. “I saw your article and thought we could talk.” 2. “I came across your website—that’s why we’re having this conversation.” That’s when I realized the power of having a strong personal website especially for PhD applications.
Eventually, I decided that a Master’s degree was the right choice for me at this stage. I chose TUM as my primary option. However, practical challenges remained. Munich is expensive, and TUM has tuition fees. With support from my parents, I decided to move forward—but visa delays forced me to defer my admission. By then, I had clarity: 1. Master’s was my path 2. TUM was my fallback This clarity gave me the confidence to apply to more competitive universities.
Since then, I’ve received offers from the University of Edinburgh and Brunel University London. I’m currently waiting on scholarship decisions through the British Council. I also received a living stipend scholarship in Germany, which may be extended to 2026 under certain conditions. At the same time, I’ve applied for a German student visa. The future feels somewhat certain. And I’ve come to accept that. Looking back, a few things made a real difference: Clarity of narrative: Aligning my projects, SOP, and goals into one coherent story Seeking feedback: Especially from people who have already gone through the process Being strategic: Financially and academically Reaching out: Professors do respond—if your work aligns Building a presence: A personal website can open unexpected doors Most importantly, I learned that where you come from does not define where you can go. I initially believed that students from my background had limited chances at top universities. But with the right strategy and persistence, that assumption proved wrong. Reminding yourself why you started—and having people around you who support you—makes all the difference.
